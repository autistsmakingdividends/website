Long’s founder argues its pairs build deeper liquidity than rivals such as Pons. Difference is Long’s cost relative to Pons’s at the same clip, so −35.7% means Long costs about a third less to exit, not 35.7 percentage points less. Long is cheaper at every size. Only the first two clear statistical significance — hover any figure for its p-value — and the last two are differences we cannot distinguish from noise.

The claim holds where it matters to most people, and only there. At a $1,000 exit 91% of Long assets stay under 5% cost, against 63% of Pons — the single widest gap on this page, and significant at p=0.0005. By $10,000 the two are level on that measure (12% vs 13%), and above $50,000 they are indistinguishable. Long’s design does not build deeper books at size; it builds a working exit for far more of its tokens at the size a normal person actually trades.

Attribution cross-validates two independent sources: Long’s factory TokenCreated log and GMGN’s board label agree exactly, once we backfilled a scan chunk that had timed out and been skipped, hiding 2,057 Long tokens in a set that read as complete.

Note 03 · Settlement review

A quote is not a fill

Every cost on this page began life as an aggregator quote, and a quote is arithmetic over pool reserves. It never runs the token’s own transfer code. A token that blocks selling, taxes it, or gates it behind a settlement whitelist therefore quotes identically to one that does not — which means until now this page could not tell them apart. So we stopped asking and started moving the tokens.

The book is mostly honest. A $1,000 clip of assets moves whole, and not one of them arrived short — there is no transfer tax anywhere in this universe that we could measure. The clip is minted to a test address by writing the contract’s own balance slot, then sent, with the destination balance read back in the same call. It is one request per asset, which is what lets it run over the whole book on every refresh rather than on a sample.

One asset refuses an ordinary transfer. Funded directly through its own balance slot, RIP reverts a wallet-to-wallet send with InvalidTransfer() at every size we tried, down to a ten-thousandth of the clip. It is rated D, so nobody was relying on it — but its liquidity quotes like anything else, which is the whole point of this note.

What this test does not do. It moves the tokens; it does not run the aggregator’s router. We built that too — the real swap calldata executed against live state — and it agreed with the quote on all 163 assets it reached, worst case 1.20%. It also took two and a half hours per pass, because this node meters eth_simulateV1 by execution cost rather than by request. That is not a price worth paying on a schedule, so it is not part of the refresh. What survives is the cheap half, which is also the half that finds things.

Two assets were briefly on the refusal list and should not have been. QUOTRON reverts with UnauthorizedPoolSettlement() and MOS with an error no public database knows — but only when the tokens are pulled out of the Uniswap v4 singleton, which is a hook doing exactly its job, and our probe was by construction the unauthorised caller. Given an ordinary holder instead, QUOTRON moves a full clip intact. MOS trades only in v4 pools, so there is no ordinary holder to ask, and it stays unmeasured. A refusal we provoked is not a finding.

And depth is measured against a float that is not always fixed. chain assets in this book expose mint(address,uint256). The loudest is HOOD, rated A on a verified $270,741 exit. Its minter is live: we simulated a mint of 1,000,000 HOOD — roughly a hundred times the entire supply — and it succeeded in one call, while the same call from any other address reverts. It is not idle either. Twenty- five percent of the supply was printed in the preceding two and a half days, and while we were measuring it the supply rose 2.83% in forty-eight minutes. The exit is real. The denominator is a rumour. Both facts now sit in the same row, because a rating that mentions only the flattering one is marketing.

The equities are a separate case, and reading their bytecode gets it wrong. Every tokenised equity here is a 283-byte beacon-proxy stub; scored on that stub, all of them looked like fixed-supply tokens. Their real implementation is 11.6 KB and carries mint, burn and pause. That is not a defect — a custodial wrapper has to mint against a deposit and burn on redemption — so they carry a different badge from HOOD. It does mean that assets on this page, the deepest ones, can have transfers halted by their issuer, which no amount of pool depth survives. We would rather say so than let AAA imply otherwise.

Method. For each asset we request routed sell quotes at five clip sizes plus a $50 reference clip, and compute impact as 1 − (out(N)/N) ÷ (out(ref)/ref) — measured against the asset’s own marginal price, so no oracle enters. Quotes settle into USDG, this chain’s dominant quote asset, falling back to native ETH. That matters: an earlier pass quoted into ETH and charged every asset the WETH/USDG hop on top of its own book, overstating AAPL at $250k as 1.46% when it is 0.45%. Prices come from ParaSwap where it routes and KyberSwap where it does not. Issuer names are read from each contract’s own name().

What a rating is not. These are point‑in‑time quotes, and depth on this chain moves with the US session; repeated passes seconds apart reproduce to the basis point, but the same asset hours apart can differ by a full percentage point. A rating measures a book, not an issuer. Pooled balance is not depth: one asset here sits on $9.7M of pooled liquidity and still rates CCC, because a pool’s balance aggregates every price range including the ones nowhere near spot. That is the entire reason this is quoted rather than computed from a liquidity figure.

Why a price feed, not our own quote. Clip sizes depend on knowing each token’s price, and that price used to be inferred from a single aggregator quote. Reliable above $1 a token — 0 of 29 equities checked were wrong — and unreliable below: 43% wrong between $0.0001 and $0.01, 69% under $0.0001, because a 6-decimal quote asset rounds a micro-priced token to a couple of raw units and one quote has nothing to check it against. AUTSM came out 196× high and CNPY eleven million times high. Prices now come from GeckoTerminal, cross-checked against GMGN — the two agree within 15% on 78% of tokens they both cover. Every row here has a sizing price corroborated by one of them; rows whose price was contradicted are withheld until re-measured.

Why this many assets, and not more. The universe is not every token on the chain. Activity was sampled from Transfer logs across eleven block windows; capture–recapture put the live population at roughly 7,100 tokens, of which 2,536 were actually observed. The scan list is every tokenised equity plus the top 1,000 non-equity tokens by transfer count, less five infrastructure addresses — 1,194 targets. An asset reaches this page only if it has a price corroborated by an independent feed and an exit curve we could actually measure. The 1,000-token cut is a budget, not a natural boundary: quiet tokens below it were never measured, and absence from this page is not a rating.

And one exclusion was our own fault. The first pass returned the same empty value for “the aggregator says there is no route” and “the request failed”, so a transient failure on the reference clip dropped an asset permanently, with nothing retrying it. It cost the book NVDA, MSFT, META, NFLX, LLY, MSTR, MU and LULU — four of which are AAA — plus eleven others. Re-measuring the 171 exclusions with the two cases held apart recovered 19. Silent survivorship is the defect this page is least entitled to have.

A quote is not a fill. Exit costs here are aggregator quotes, and a quote is arithmetic over pool reserves — it never executes the token’s transfer code. Every asset is therefore also moved: a $1,000 clip is minted to a test address through a storage override on the contract’s own balance slot (probed, never assumed) and transferred, with the destination balance read in the same call by an injected probe contract. Rated assets go further and run the aggregator’s real router calldata through eth_simulateV1 against live state, comparing the USDG that arrives to the USDG that was promised. Where the test could not be funded the asset is marked unmeasured — never clean. An early version of this harness funded from Uniswap v4 pool IDs, which hold nothing, and reported a blue-chip equity as a total loss; the rule that a short-funded test is void, not failed, comes from that.

Pool reserves are not depth. The reserves column is what GeckoTerminal reports sitting in an asset’s pools, and it is deliberately next to what the asset can actually absorb, so the gap is visible. SPACEHOOD holds $1.4M of reserves and breaks on a $6,418 sell. YARD holds $1.03M and cannot clear $1,000. A pool’s balance counts every price range, including the ones nowhere near spot. That gap is the entire reason this page quotes rather than counts.
